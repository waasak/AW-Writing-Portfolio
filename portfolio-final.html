From Home Kitchen to Bull City Institution

AP-Style Feature

A reported profile on chef Preeti Waas and Cheeni's move from a Raleigh counter-service cafe to a full Durham dining room.

Preeti Waas didn't plan to become a restaurateur. She planned to stop eating dinner alone.

Feature — Food & Culture

DURHAM, North Carolina — Preeti Waas didn't plan to become a restaurateur. She planned to stop eating dinner alone.

In 2011, Waas moved to North Carolina's Research Triangle Park with her husband and two daughters. By 2022, both daughters had left home, and Waas, facing an empty kitchen table, opened Cheeni Indian Food Emporium, a small counter-service cafe in north Raleigh built around the regional dishes she grew up eating in India — food she once doubted her own ability to cook.

The cafe found an audience quickly. The James Beard Foundation named Waas a semifinalist for Best Chef: Southeast in 2023, then again in 2024. By then, Cheeni had outgrown its original space.

Last year, on her birthday, Waas opened a second Cheeni in downtown Durham, inside a larger space on Corcoran Street with an open kitchen and room for a full sit-down menu — lunch, brunch and dinner, built around the same instinct that started the first location: cook what she knows, and feed whoever shows up.

The Durham location operates as more than a restaurant. Alongside dinner service, the kitchen doubles as a teaching space and community hub, hosting classes and collaborations. Next door, Bar Beej — a Bollywood-themed cocktail bar run by Waas' daughter, Amy — earned its own James Beard semifinalist nod for Best New Bar.

Three James Beard nods in, Waas said the recognition still catches her off guard. What hasn't changed is the reason she started cooking in the first place: to build a table people wanted to sit at.

What My Grandmother's Kitchen Taught Me About Words

Personal Essay

My grandmother never measured anything. She cooked the way some people talk — by feel, correcting as she went, trusting that she'd know the right amount when she saw it. I spent years trying to pin her recipes down into something I could follow, writing "a pinch" and "until it looks right" into a notebook that made sense to no one but me.

It took me a long time to realize she'd already taught me how to write.

Good copy isn't measured out, either. You can follow every rule of grammar and structure and still end up with something nobody wants to read — over-salted with jargon, under-seasoned with anything a person would actually say out loud. The best sentences, like the best meals, are adjusted at the last minute. A word swapped because it sounds better in the mouth. A line cut because the dish doesn't need it.

I think about her kitchen every time I edit something down to its final draft — not because I'm looking for the poetry in it, but because she taught me the most useful lesson a writer can learn: trust the version that tastes right, even when you can't fully explain why.

These days, when a client asks me why I moved a sentence, or cut a paragraph that technically had good information in it, I still don't always have a tidy answer. I just know it reads better now. My grandmother would understand completely.
